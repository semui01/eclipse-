*Eclipses* often come in *pairs* during an eclipse season.
But what actually *links* a solar eclipse to a lunar one?

We don't get them *monthly* because the Moon's orbit is *tilted*,
usually *missing* Earth's shadow.

Take the solar eclipse of *August 2026*.
It's followed by a *lunar eclipse* two weeks later.

The Moon's tilted path crosses Earth's flat plane at *two* specific intersection points.
But just crossing the plane *isn't enough*.
The *Sun* has to line up perfectly with these points.

This precise alignment creates a temporary window known as an *eclipse season*.
As Earth orbits, this window stays open for *34 days*. || As Earth orbits, this window stays open for thirty-four days.
Now, compare that to the Moon's *speed*.
It takes *14.7 days* to reach the other side. || It takes fourteen point seven days to reach the other side.

If the Moon triggers a *solar eclipse* at the first point,
it just keeps moving along its orbit.
Two weeks later, that *34-day window* is still active, || Two weeks later, that thirty-four day window is still active,
allowing the Moon to perfectly hit the *second point* for a *lunar eclipse*.

So that August 2026 solar eclipse *guarantees* the lunar eclipse two weeks later.
